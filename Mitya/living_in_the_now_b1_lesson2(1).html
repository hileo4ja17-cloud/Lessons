Present simple vs present continuous

Two tenses, two jobs. One talks about your routine & facts, the other about right now & temporary things.

Present simple

AffirmativeI/You/We/They understand.
He/She/It understands.
NegativeI/You/We/They don't understand.
He/She/It doesn't understand.
QuestionDo I/you/we/they understand?
Does he/she/it understand?
Short ans.Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
Yes, he does. / No, he doesn't.

Present continuous

Affirmativesubject + am/are/is + verb-ing
We're working.
Negativesubject + am not/aren't/isn't + verb-ing
She isn't working.
QuestionAm/Are/Is + subject + verb-ing?
Are they working?
Short ans.Yes, I am. / No, they aren't.
  • Present simple → habits & routines (We walk to school every day.), permanent situations (They live in France.), general & scientific facts (Most birds fly.).
  • Present continuous → actions happening now (She's studying for an exam.) and temporary situations (Jim is studying in the UK.).
⏳ Time signals for present simple: always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never, once/twice a week, on Mondays.
⚡ For present continuous: now, right now, at the moment, today, Look!, Listen!

State & action verbs

Some verbs describe an action (things we do). Others describe a state: feelings, opinions, thoughts, senses. State verbs are not usually used in the continuous.

✅ We sayI understand now. · I love this song. · She needs help.
🚫 NotI am understanding · I am loving · She is needing
🧠 Common state verbs: like, love, hate, prefer, want, need, know, understand, believe, remember, mean, seem, care.
🔁 A few can be both: think (opinion = state / consider = action), have (own = state / have breakfast = action), see (with eyes = state / meet = action).

Noun suffixes -ment · -ion · -ence

A suffix is added to the end of a word and often changes its type — turning a verb or an adjective into a noun.

argumentargue (verb) → argument (noun)
permissionpermit (verb) → permission (noun)
differencedifferent (adj.) → difference (noun)
✍️ Spelling often changes! permit → permission · solve → solution · confident → confidence · adolescent → adolescence. Add, but adjust.

How English grows new words

English rarely invents brand-new words — it recycles them. Add a suffix to the end to change the word class; add a prefix to the front to change the meaning. Here's the big picture.

verb→noun
-ment · -tion · -ence · -er
govern→government, educate→education, differ→difference, teach→teacher
adj.→noun
-ness · -ity · -ence
kind→kindness, able→ability, confident→confidence
noun→verb
-ise/-ize · -en · -ify
modern→modernise, wide→widen, beauty→beautify
noun→adj.
-ful · -less · -ous · -y
care→careful/careless, danger→dangerous, rain→rainy
adj.→adverb
-ly
quick→quickly, happy→happily
prefix→new meaning
un- · dis- · re- · im-/in-
happy→unhappy, agree→disagree, do→redo, possible→impossible
🔑 Big idea: a suffix usually changes what kind of word it is (verb→noun→adjective→adverb). A prefix usually changes the meaning (often to the opposite) but keeps the word class.
